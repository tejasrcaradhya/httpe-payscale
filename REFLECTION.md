# Reflection: HTTPE — PayScale

*Draft reflection — written from the perspective of the person completing this project. Personalize this before submitting; it should sound like you, not like a template.*

The hardest part of this project wasn't any single technical decision — it was resisting the urge to treat every requirement as independent, when in reality almost every choice constrained the ones that came after it. Picking Kafka on Day 2 wasn't really "done" until Day 7 worked out actual partition counts from throughput math, and even then, Day 14's review caught that an earlier illustrative number (32 partitions, written before that math existed) had been left sitting in two documents after the real figure (64) was calculated. That was a useful reminder that a 15-day project produces a lot of surface area for documents to quietly disagree with each other, and that "integration and polish" isn't busywork — it's where you actually catch that.

The trade-off I keep coming back to is the sharding decision. It would have been easy to pick `hash(account_id)`, show the distribution-uniformity math, and call it done — the simulation genuinely does show near-perfect balance across shards. But the ARB's own sample question about a single merchant processing 40% of transactions exposes exactly what that uniformity claim doesn't cover: *account* distribution being even says nothing about *traffic* distribution being even. The fix wasn't a cleverer sharding scheme — you can't split one account's row across shards without breaking the OCC version model — it was realizing the batch settlement mechanism (already needed for FR-006, for completely separate reasons) happened to be the actual answer, because it means the merchant's own balance row doesn't need a synchronous update on every incoming payment. That connection wasn't obvious to me until I'd already designed both pieces separately.

The concurrency control work taught me something about the difference between "this works" and "this is provably correct." I could have described OCC in a paragraph and moved on. Actually working through the exact write-skew scenario from the training material — two transactions racing to debit the same account past its balance — and tracing through why the version check catches it, step by step, was a different kind of exercise than describing the pattern in the abstract. I think that's the difference the ARB is actually probing for with a question like "prove your OCC implementation prevents this" — not whether I can name the pattern, but whether I actually traced through what happens.

The FMEA was the most humbling part. My first instinct was to list the failure modes I already knew about — dead database nodes, network partitions, the usual infrastructure suspects. It was only when I forced myself to fill out the "data" and "operational" categories properly that I found the genuinely dangerous entries: a bad migration silently corrupting balances, or — worse — an alert rule that's broken in a way that hides its own failure. Those don't feel like "infrastructure" problems, and I don't think I'd have found them if the category requirement hadn't made me look somewhere I wouldn't have thought to look on my own.

If I did this again, I'd want to actually run at least one of the load tests against a real deployed sandbox rather than leaving all three at the pseudocode stage — the correctness proofs are sound, but there's a difference between an argument and a measurement.
